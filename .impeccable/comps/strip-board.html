Squirrel

The strip board

Every thing you told Squirrel is a printed strip in a holder. The holder's colour says which bay it belongs to; the words on it are yours; the marks in the margin are Buddy's. Nothing is a message and nothing is a card.

Squirrel choosing is a physical act: it pulls one strip half out of its rack, which is the whole of what an offer is. Answering a strip strikes it and slides it into the day's tray, and the tray is empty again tomorrow.

Fonts and colours are the ones you pinned. Everything else — material, composition, controls, motion — is new. Sample words are authored.

The contract

Built from code rather than from a rendered comp, so the ambition is written down here and the finish is audited against it.

World
Air-traffic flight progress strips. Printed slips in coloured holders, racked in bays, annotated by hand, pulled out when they need attention and removed when they are done. A controller's board carries no totals and no history — what has left the airspace is off the board, which is this product's oldest rule wearing somebody else's uniform.
First viewport
An operations header with the mark, the clock and the day. Under it, one strip pulled proud of everything else: what Squirrel picked, the rule that picked it printed above the words, three stamps down its right edge. Below that, four racks running to the bottom of the screen, each headed by an enamel bay sign and opening with a blank strip you can write on. Along the foot, the day's tray: what left the board today, struck through, the newest still recoverable.
Visitor path
Read the pulled strip. Stamp it, or leave it. Drop your eyes to a rack and work the strips in front of you. Type on a blank strip when a thought arrives. Stop whenever — the board looks the same whether you did four or none, because nothing on it counts.
Signature interaction
Cocking a strip. A strip lifts 3px out of its rack when it has your attention and stays there while it is the one being worked; that is how a controller marks a flight that needs something, and it replaces every highlight, badge and selected state in the product. The pulled strip at the top is the same gesture at full size.
Motion grammar
Everything moves along the rack's own axes and nothing fades. A strip lifts on the vertical, an answered strip strikes then travels sideways into the tray, and a new strip enters by pushing the rack down one row. The travel starts after the 1150ms the undo needs, so the strike is visible before the strip goes. Reduced motion keeps the strike and the tray and drops the travel.
Cross-surface reach
The strip is the unit everywhere: one bay at a time on a phone, one strip per row in the evening message, and the same holder colours in Campfire when a thing is named there. A future surface inherits the rack rather than a page layout.
Honest risk
Strips are close to cards, so the rethink lives in the rules rather than the silhouette: dense rows, no chips, no bubbles, state by mark rather than by fill. If those slip, this reads as the old world with sharper corners. The second risk is density — a board of 14pt rows is harder on a bad day than four big cards, and the pulled strip is the mitigation.

The bay signs replace navigation

Four signs, one lit. There is no menu, no rail and no back button: you are always looking at one rack of one board.

The pulled strip does not scroll away

It sits above the bays because it belongs to none of them. When you stamp it, the next one is pulled — or nothing is, and the space closes.

Writing is a blank strip

Always the first row of the rack you are in, so a thought lands in the bay you are looking at. The camera lives on the same row.

What this costs

Strips are dense, and dense is harder than big soft cards when you are wiped. The mitigations are the pulled strip and the check-in: a wiped reading should thin the board to the pulled strip and the tray, and that is a rule to write into the picker rather than a nicety.

What each part of the old app became

Nothing in PRODUCT.md changed. The whole redesign is a change of material and of where state lives.

The turn
A strip. It does not scroll away, it does not repeat, and it carries no timestamp of when it was said — only the mark that matters: when it arrived, when it is due, or what time it happens.
The card
Retired with its 3px outline, 14px corner and drop shadow. A strip is 2px, square-cornered, and sits in a holder — the outline language stays, the sticker does not.
The chip
A stamp: rectangular, keyed, and always in the same place on the object it acts on.
The dock
The blank strip at the head of each rack.
Buddy's bubble
A pencil line in the strip's margin, in the ink colour, with at most one underlined action. He never occupies space of his own.
The offer
The pulled strip. Choosing is now a physical act you can see from across the room, and the rule that chose it is printed on the strip.
The doors and the rail
Bay signs. Enamel, always visible, and the count on them is what is in the rack rather than what you owe.
Undo
The tray. A strip that leaves the board lands there struck through, and PUT IT BACK is a stamp like any other until the tray empties overnight.

The shelf is the lip under the rack

What you kept and what you set aside are not a fifth bay and never were. They are the ledge at the foot of the notes rack, past every strip you have not decided about, and reaching one turns that rack into the shelf.

Why a ledge and not a bay

A fifth sign on the board would put stalled work back in front of you every morning, which is the thing setting something aside exists to stop. The ledge is inside the notes rack and below every live strip, so you reach it by going past what you have not decided rather than by being shown it.

A shelf never counts

The sign carries the name and no number, and this is the one place in the product where that is a rule rather than a choice. A number beside stalled work is a reproach, and the point of setting something aside is to stop being asked about it.

No blank strip

Where every rack opens with something to write on, a shelf opens with the way back. Nothing is kept or set aside by being typed — both states are reached by deciding about a strip that already exists, so a shelf that could be written into would be lying about where its contents come from.

Two different objects

Set aside is recessed and dashed: present, and not a thing you can pick up. Kept is flat printed stock with no shadow and no mark — a fact rather than a job. Neither can be mistaken for a live strip lying in a rack, which is the whole point of them being off the board.

The holder still says where it came from

A held task keeps its amber holder and a held note its purple one, so the shelf can hold both without a second label. What would move it takes the mark's place, in his own words: when he replies, when the part arrives, someday — the three ways to say you cannot act, kept apart because they end differently.
